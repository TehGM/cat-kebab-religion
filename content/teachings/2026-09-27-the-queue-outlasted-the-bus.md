The queue at the eastbound stop was nine deep when the screen announced the 14 as cancelled, and did not become eight. Nobody opened a rival app. Nobody looked about for a taxi. A woman near the front took her phone out, read the word, and put it away again without comment, which two people behind her noticed and only one thought worth mentioning afterwards.

The screen had already said the 14 was coming in six minutes, and then in six minutes again, twice, before it gave up the pretence and used the word cancelled outright. The queue held its shape through all three announcements: nine people, in the order they had joined, on a strip of pavement between a bollard and a verge of gravel that had once hoped for tarmac and had, by all appearances, made its peace with the alternative. Nobody sat on the bollard. Nobody complained to the gravel either.

{{% pullquote %}}The queue was not being patient. It had simply not occurred to it to be anything else.{{% /pullquote %}}

The 22 arrived nineteen minutes later, going the long way round, and the queue boarded it in the order it had kept the whole time, without incident and without a word to the driver about where the 14 had got to. Br. Anselm, who had been timing the wait on principle and not from any personal need to travel, logged it afterwards as forty-three minutes, door to door, and correct to the second.

The Church has no ruling to offer on the 14, which is a matter for the operator and not for Us. What it will say is this: a queue capable of holding its order for forty-three minutes, on a corner with nothing to recommend it and a bus that kept not coming, was — whether it knew it or not — closer in that half hour to the whole of the faith than most sermons manage in twice the time.
