The bread is chosen before it is worn, and this is the step most people try to skip. Not the heel, which folds wrong at the ears. Not the thin, machine-sliced kind, which tears the moment a whisker leans on it. What is wanted is a slice with a day's age on it and some structure left — firm enough to hold a shape, soft enough not to crack across a face.

It goes on indoors, without apology, and stays on for whoever happens to call round — a rule kept by example rather than by decree, since before there was a decree to keep it by. The kitchen floor has become the customary place to be photographed in it, on nobody's instruction; a kitchen floor asks nothing of a visitor and photographs adequately, and that has proved sufficient qualification.

  do="Press the slice to the face slowly, ears found after, and stand still a moment before doing anything else."

The Church keeps a short account of how it has gone since:

{{< doctrine >}}
Removed for a guest | Not observed once.
Removed in anger | Also not observed; the bread has done nothing to deserve it.
Worn to a wedding | Three times, and once to a tribunal.
Lost over water | Twice, and returned by dawn both times, which nobody has explained.
{{< /doctrine >}}

None of this is claimed to do anything. The bread keeps nobody warm, feeds nobody, and stops being properly edible somewhere around the second wearing, which no one on record has raised as an objection. What it does, reliably, is cost a person exactly as long as it takes to put a slice of bread on without hurrying the job — which turns out, on reflection, to be most of what has ever actually been asked.
