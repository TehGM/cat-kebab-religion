Nine paces. That is the distance from the bread bin to the kettle in the kitchen where the custom began, and the Church has never found a reason to make it longer. A slice is chosen, worn, and walked across the lino at the speed of someone carrying something that is not theirs. At the kettle it stops. This is the whole of the observance, and the Church is surprised each year by how many people try to add to it.

They add a second kettle. They add the hallway. One brother, whom the Church will not name, wore his slice as far as the bus stop and was asked by the driver what it was for. He said it was for the kettle. The driver let him on regardless, which the Church does not count as a ruling, but notes.

{{< litany label="Said at the bin, then at the kettle" >}}
Bread, chosen | and carried.
Bread, worn | and not adjusted.
Kettle, reached | and that is far enough.
{{< /litany >}}

At the kettle the slice is not removed. It is allowed to watch the water come to the boil, as He has been seen to watch things, without comment. When the switch clicks, the walk is over, and the slice may be put down on the nearest dry surface with both hands, as one sets down a sleeping thing.

{{% commentary %}}
**On the extra step —** There is always one. Do not take it. *(Br. Tomasz)*

**On the lino —** Cold, and not part of the teaching. *(Sr. Aniela, disputed)*
{{% /commentary %}}

The Church observes that nine paces is not far, and that this is the point. A thing carried exactly as far as it was meant to go has been carried well. Nobody has yet found a better account of the Prophet's own journeys, which are longer, but are kept to the same rule.
